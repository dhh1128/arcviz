## What is verified (primary)

UNCTAD's own map disclaimer states the encoding directly:

> "Dashed lines represent undefined borders. Dotted line represents approximately the Line of Control in Jammu and Kashmir agreed upon by India and Pakistan."

> "The final status of the following territories has not yet been agreed or determined: Abyei area, Aksai Chin, Arunachal Pradesh, Bi'r Tawil, Hala'ib Triangle, Ilemi Triangle, Jammu and Kashmir, Kuril Islands, Paracel Islands, Scarborough Shoal, Senkaku Islands and Spratly Islands."

The same page separately flags: a named Argentina–United Kingdom sovereignty dispute over "the Falkland Islands (Malvinas)"; the Sudan–South Sudan boundary as "undetermined"; and the Chagos Archipelago presented "without prejudice to the question of sovereignty" (this last phrase is quoted as it appeared in the fetched page content).

(https://unctad.org/map-disclaimer, retrieved 2026-09-14)

## What is not independently verified (leads, from search-engine synthesis only)

- A WebSearch summary attributes to the U.S. State Department's LSIB dataset documentation a

## Assessment: is "disputed boundary" a genuine third epistemic state, or a variant of binary?

Inference (ours): Based on what is confirmed above, disputed/undefined boundary symbology is
**not** a third epistemic state comparable to CATZOC's multi-grade confidence ladder or to
arcviz's three target states. UNCTAD's own rule is binary at the line level: **solid = agreed
border, dashed/dotted = not agreed** (with a further sub-case — dotted specifically for an
agreed *cessation line*, i.e. the Kashmir Line of Control, which is a boundary both sides
agree marks a ceasefire/administrative line without agreeing it is the international border).
So there are really two things being conflated in casual descriptions of "the disputed-border
convention":

1. **Existence/location is contested** (dashed — "we don't know/agree where this line truly
   is," e.g. most of the UNCTAD list) — an *epistemic* uncertainty about where a real thing is.
2. **A line everyone can point to exists and is followed for practical purposes, but its legal
   status is unsettled** (dotted, per UNCTAD's Kashmir LoC example) — this is closer to a
   *labeling* or *status* uncertainty than a *positional* one: the geometry is not in doubt,
   only what to call it.

Neither of these is close to arcviz's three HP1 states (withheld contents / missing referent /
blinded edge), because a disputed boundary is a case where **multiple parties each claim full
knowledge and disagree** — it's a knowledge *conflict*, not a knowledge *gap*. Arcviz's problem
is entirely about gaps (something is known to be missing, or known to be there but
unreadable), not about competing claims. The honest conclusion for this assignment: **disputed
boundaries are the weakest of the four researched analogues for arcviz's specific problem** —
useful only as a cautionary contrast (a reminder that "uncertain" in cartography sometimes
means "contested," not "hidden," and conflating the two would mislabel arcviz's design intent).
CATZOC and the unsurveyed-area symbol (see the other two capture files) are structurally much
closer matches.
