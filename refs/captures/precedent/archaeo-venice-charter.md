# Venice Charter (1964) — captured primary text

**Title**: International Charter for the Conservation and Restoration of Monuments and Sites (the "Venice Charter")
**Body**: IInd International Congress of Architects and Technicians of Historic Monuments, Venice, 1964; adopted by ICOMOS 1965.
**URL (official ICOMOS host)**: https://www.icomos.org/images/DOCUMENTS/Charters/venice_e.pdf
**Retrieved**: 2026-09-14
**Status**: PRIMARY — full text extracted directly from the official ICOMOS-hosted PDF (fetched via WebFetch, parsed via the Read tool's PDF support; ICOMOS.org is the charter's own custodian body).

## Full verbatim text of the load-bearing articles

### Article 9 (Restoration)
> The process of restoration is a highly specialized operation. Its aim is to preserve and reveal the aesthetic and historic value of the monument and is based on respect for original material and authentic documents. It must stop at the point where conjecture begins, and in this case moreover any extra work which is indispensable must be distinct from the architectural composition and must bear a contemporary stamp. The restoration in any case must be preceded and followed by an archaeological and historical study of the monument.

### Article 11 (unity of style / superimposed periods)
> The valid contributions of all periods to the building of a monument must be respected, since unity of style is not the aim of a restoration. When a building includes the superimposed work of different periods, the revealing of the underlying state can only be justified in exceptional circumstances and when what is removed is of little interest and the material which is brought to light is of great historical, archaeological or aesthetic value, and its state of preservation good enough to justify the action. Evaluation of the importance of the elements involved and the decision as to what may be destroyed cannot rest solely on the individual in charge of the work.

### Article 12 (Replacements) — the "distinguishable" clause
> Replacements of missing parts must integrate harmoniously with the whole, but at the same time must be distinguishable from the original so that restoration does not falsify the artistic or historic evidence.

### Article 13 (Additions)
> Additions cannot be allowed except in so far as they do not detract from the interesting parts of the building, its traditional setting, the balance of its composition and its relation with its surroundings.

### Article 15 (Excavations — the anastylosis clause)
> Excavations should be carried out in accordance with scientific standards and the recommendation defining international principles to be applied in the case of archaeological excavation adopted by UNESCO in 1956.
>
> Ruins must be maintained and measures necessary for the permanent conservation and protection of architectural features and of objects discovered must be taken. Furthermore, every means must be taken to facilitate the understanding of the monument and to reveal it without ever distorting its meaning.
>
> All reconstruction work should however be ruled out "a priori". Only anastylosis, that is to say, the reassembling of existing but dismembered parts can be permitted. The material used for integration should always be recognizable and its use should be the least that will ensure the conservation of a monument and the reinstatement of its form.

### Article 16 (Publication / documentation)
> In all works of preservation, restoration or excavation, there should always be precise documentation in the form of analytical and critical reports, illustrated with drawings and photographs. Every stage of the work of clearing, consolidation, rearrangement and integration, as well as technical and formal features identified during the course of the work, should be included. This record should be placed in the archives of a public institution and made available to research workers. It is recommended that the report should be published.

## What it requires (established fact, not inference)

1. **Anastylosis only** — reassembly of the monument's OWN dismembered original parts. Any material added to fill a gap (Article 15's "material used for integration") is new fabric, not reconstruction, and must be "always recognizable" — i.e. legible as an addition without needing the archive record.
2. **Restoration stops where conjecture begins** (Article 9) — a hard boundary. Past that boundary, any indispensable extra work must be (a) distinct from the surrounding architectural composition and (b) "bear a contemporary stamp" — deliberately of-its-own-time, not a stylistic forgery of the original period.
3. **Distinguishability is permanent, not just contemporaneous** (Article 12) — replacements "must be distinguishable from the original SO THAT restoration does not falsify the artistic or historic evidence." The reasoning given is evidentiary integrity, not aesthetics: an indistinguishable repair is a falsification of the historical record, full stop, regardless of who's looking or when.
4. **Article 16 is the fallback, not the primary control** — documentation (reports, drawings, photographs, archived and ideally published) is REQUIRED in addition to Articles 9/12/15, not instead of them. The charter does not accept "it's written down somewhere" as a substitute for the object itself carrying its own evidentiary honesty.

## Design principle — the honesty-to-a-future-observer rationale

The charter's own stated reasoning for distinguishability (Article 12) is explicit: **"must be distinguishable from the original so that restoration does not falsify the artistic or historic evidence."** The load-bearing word is *falsify* — an indistinguishable repair doesn't just look inauthentic to a scholar in the room at the time; it corrupts the physical evidence itself for everyone who encounters the monument afterward, including someone with zero access to the excavation/restoration archive. Article 16's documentation requirement exists as a second line of defense for exactly the population Article 12 is already worried about (future researchers), which confirms the concern is trans-generational, not "will today's visitor be fooled."

## Known limits / disputes (flagged as such)

- **The charter is contested as dated.** WebSearch summary (secondary, from Wikipedia/general commentary, not independently verified against a scholarly source here): the Venice Charter is now "seen by some as outdated, representing Modernist views opposed to reconstruction," and UNESCO has since "cautiously accepted" reconstruction in exceptional circumstances tied to sustaining cultural/intangible practice. This is a LEAD, not confirmed against a primary UNESCO document in this research pass.
- **Translation dispute on Article 9.** A WebSearch result cited a paper title, "The 'open' Venice Charter: Learning from the Multiple Interpretations and Translations of the Charter's Article 9" (ResearchGate), and noted the English/French versions of Article 9 have been argued to diverge subtly (the French "Elle s'arrête là où commence l'hypothèse" for "It must stop at the point where conjecture begins"). Not fetched/verified in full — LEAD only.
