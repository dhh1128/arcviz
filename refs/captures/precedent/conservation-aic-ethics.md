## The clause (Guidelines for Practice, Article/Guideline 23, "Compensation for Loss")

> "Compensation for Loss: Any intervention to compensate for loss should be documented in
> treatment records and reports and should be detectable by common examination methods. Such
> compensation should be reversible and should not falsely modify the known aesthetic,
> conceptual, and physical characteristics of the cultural property, especially by removing or
> obscuring original material."

This single clause carries three distinct obligations relevant to arcviz's HP1 problem:
1. **Detectability**: "detectable by common examination methods" — the loss-compensation must
   be identifiable as such without special tools, on ordinary inspection.
2. **Reversibility**: the fill must not become permanently fused with / indistinguishable from
   the original.
3. **Non-falsification**: compensation "should not falsely modify the known aesthetic,
   conceptual, and physical characteristics" — i.e., it must not misrepresent what is actually
   known vs. supplied.

## Historical origin

The AIC Code traces to the "Report of the Murray Pease Committee," adopted by the IIC-American
Group (predecessor to AIC) on 1963-06-08 in New York, and published in *Studies in
Conservation*, August 1964, 9(3):116-121 — i.e., the reversibility/detectability doctrine in the
US professional code is contemporaneous with (within a year of) Brandi's 1963 *Teoria del
Restauro* in Italy. Not claiming direct causal influence — flagging as an interesting
coincidence in timing, not a sourced claim of one document citing the other.
