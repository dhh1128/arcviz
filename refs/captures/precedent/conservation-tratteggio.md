## What it is

Tratteggio (also "rigatino") is a loss-compensation technique developed in the mid-1940s by
Cesare Brandi as director of the Istituto Centrale del Restauro (ICR) in Rome, born from the
need to reintegrate paintings destroyed in WWII (e.g. Mantegna's frescoes in the Ovetari Chapel,
Padua) "without deceiving the viewer about the extent of the damage." The loss is filled with
fine vertical hatching/strokes, in a palette usually restricted to a few base colors, arranged so
that from normal viewing distance the eye optically blends them into a harmonious color field,
but up close each stroke is visible as a discrete, non-continuous mark — "no tools are required
to detect and identify the conservator's intervention."

## Why distinguishability at close range is doctrine, not style

Brandi's theoretical grounding (Gestalt psychology, from *Teoria del Restauro*, 1963): an
unfilled lacuna doesn't just remove information locally — it "asserts itself as a form,"
dragging the eye's attention away from the image as a whole ("the lacuna places itself
aggressively in the foreground while the image slides into the background"). A correct fill
should invert this again, letting the lacuna "recede into the background" perceptually — but the
technique must stop short of literally reconstructing lost brushwork, because that would let the
restorer's invention pass as the artist's hand.

Quote (Occelli, "The tradition of Brandi," trans. Mary Ann Macintosh, citing Brandi 1949: 698,
via capture `conservation-brandi-principles.md`):
> "to keep any integration within the strictest limitations, with practical implementations that
> are recognizable to everyone, so as not to use the hypothetical reintegration to usurp the
> authenticity which belongs only to the ways in which it was created"

and (citing Brandi 1950a: 11-12):
> "there is once again emphasis on ... the importance of the possibility of distinguishing
> between a new contribution and that which was pre-existent"

This is the doctrinal core: reintegration is permitted, but only if it remains recognizable as
reintegration — the fill must never be mistaken for original material, at the viewing distance
where it matters (close inspection).
