## Cluster 1: Credential and consent UX in digital identity

**[korir2022wallet]** Maina Korir, Simon Parkin, Paul Dunphy, "An Empirical Study of a Decentralized Identity Wallet: Usability, Security, and Perspectives on User Control," SOUPS 2022. A contextual-inquiry and interview study (30 participants) of a prototype decentralized-identity wallet.

What it establishes, read from the paper directly: participants frequently misread the mere *presence* of a cryptographic-looking identifier as itself being the security mechanism. "we noted multiple assertions that the randomized alphanumeric composition of the Decentralized Identifier (DID) represents the secure encoding or encryption of personal information. Of course, this was not true, yet the perceived presence of cryptography provided a sense of confidence and comfort." (§7.1, p. 205) Separately, participants given fine-grained selective-disclosure control reported feeling personally at fault when they disclosed more than necessary, and at least one participant believed they had no real ability to decline a requested field at all: "Nadon et al. also noticed the potential for users to feel personal failure if they over-shared once given control" (§6, p. 203); and (P5, same section): "If [Bank of] Carpathia wanted to know exactly how much I make a month, they would ask for it. And I wouldn't be able ... to say no."

What it constrains about our design: this is direct, primary evidence against two temptations arcviz should resist. First, do not let an identifier's *form* — an AID or SAID rendered as a plausible-looking random string — read as a verification signal by itself; Korir's participants did exactly that with DIDs, and it was false comfort. This is the strongest empirical grounding this stream found for the "substitution must be visible" and "provenance honesty" principles sketched in `docs/research/PLAN.md`: an entviz pill has to carry a genuinely legible verified/unverified distinction, because the raw string will otherwise be read as proof of nothing in particular, or worse, as proof of security. Second, a selective-disclosure control is not self-explanatory: giving users a toggle for what to share does not mean they will correctly understand what withholding a field costs them, or that they can decline a request at all. A disclosure UI has to be evaluated for whether it actually communicates control, not just for whether it offers it.

**[sartor2022loveatfirstsight]** — *lead, not verified.* Sartor, Sedlmeir, Rieger, Roth, "Love at First Sight? A User Experience Study of Self-Sovereign Identity Wallets," ECIS 2022. Reported by secondary summary (not read) as a UEQ-based comparison of four SSI wallets. The paper's AISeL PDF endpoint returned an HTML page rather than a document during this survey's retrieval attempt. Left as a lead; not cited for any claim.

**mDL over-disclosure comprehension: no verifiable source found.** This stream searched specifically for an empirical, human-subjects study of whether users understand what is and is not disclosed during an ISO/IEC 18013-5 mDL presentation — the brief's explicit ask — and did not find one. What surfaced instead was standards documentation (AAMVA implementation guidelines, NIST NCCoE material) describing the selective-disclosure *mechanism*, and marketing/explainer content asserting the privacy benefit as a design intent rather than a measured outcome. **We should not claim, on the strength of anything found in this survey, that mDL users understand what they have and have not disclosed** — the literature searched here contains no measurement of that either way. If such a study exists, it was not surfaced by this search; if a later stream finds one, it should supersede this note.

## Cluster 2: Security indicators and their failure

This cluster is the discipline the brief asked for on any claim that "the user will see that it is unverified," and reading the four canonical papers primarily, in order, tells a more specific story than "users ignore security indicators."

**[schechter2007emperor]** Schechter, Dhamija, Ozment, Fischer, "The Emperor's New Security Indicators," IEEE S&P 2007. Field study with real bank customers: "no participants withheld their passwords when [HTTPS] indicators were removed. [...] even when we removed [site-authentication images], 23 of the 25 (92%) participants who used their own accounts entered their passwords." (Abstract, p. 51) This is a passive-indicator failure: an indicator that is merely *absent* is not noticed, not merely disbelieved.

**[dhamija2006phishing]** Dhamija, Tygar, Hearst, "Why Phishing Works," CHI 2006. "23% of the participants did not look at browser-based cues such as the address bar, status bar and the security indicators, leading to incorrect choices 40% of the time. [...] some visual deception attacks can fool even the most sophisticated users." (Abstract, p. 581) This adds that even the users who do look can be fooled by a well-executed counterfeit — the failure is not purely inattention.

**[akhawe2013warningland]** Akhawe, Felt, "Alice in Warningland," USENIX Security 2013. This paper complicates the first two, and is the one that should discipline how far we generalize from them: click-through on modern *active* warnings varies enormously by design, from roughly a tenth of Firefox malware/phishing warnings bypassed to 70.2% of Chrome's SSL warnings. "This demonstrates that security warnings can be effective in practice; security experts and system architects should not dismiss the goal of communicating security information to end users." (Abstract, p. 257) The 2006/2007 studies evaluated *passive* indicators (a lock icon, an image) whose removal produces no interruption; this paper evaluates *interstitial* warnings that block the task. The two are not interchangeable evidence.

**[felt2015sslwarnings]** Felt et al., "Improving SSL Warnings: Comprehension and Adherence," CHI 2015. Google's own team, iterating on warning design with warning-literature best practices applied deliberately, still could not produce a well-understood warning: "We ultimately failed at our goal of a well-understood warning. [...] overall comprehension rates remained low for all warning texts." (Abstract, p. 2893) They did move *adherence* (behavior) substantially — "nearly 30% more total users chose to remain safe" — via what they call opinionated design (visual cues promoting a recommended action), while comprehension of *why* stayed low.

**What this cluster constrains about our design, taken together:** the reliable finding across all four is that a *passive* signal — an icon that is present when things are fine and merely missing or subtly different when they are not — is not adequate to convey "unverified," "redacted," or "absent." Schechter and Dhamija both show that a missing indicator does not register. What Akhawe/Felt add is a genuine way out, but a narrower one than "warn the user": an *interruptive, opinionated* treatment can change behavior (adherence) even where it does not achieve genuine understanding (comprehension). This means arcviz cannot rely on a badge, a muted color, or a small icon change to communicate "this credential is unverified" or "this node is redacted" — those are exactly the passive-indicator pattern the 2006/2007 studies show fails. It also means that if arcviz's answer is to interrupt (a modal, a required acknowledgment, a blocking state before revealing a raw field), we can expect that to change what people *do*, per Felt, but we should not claim it will make them *understand* why — comprehension and adherence are separable goals, and design that hits one does not automatically hit the other.

## Cluster 3: Graph and DAG readability

**[ghoniem2005readability]** Ghoniem, Fekete, Castagliola, "On the Readability of Graphs Using Node-Link and Matrix-Based Representations," extended version of INFOVIS 2004 / Information Visualization 4(2), 2005. A controlled experiment across seven generic graph tasks, node-link diagrams vs. adjacency-matrix representations, at varying size and density.

Headline finding, read from the paper: matrix representations outperform node-link on most tasks once a graph exceeds about twenty vertices — except path-finding. "Only for "findPath" task did node-link diagrams prove superior to matrix-based representations, although their performance deteriorates on large and dense graphs. [...] As seen earlier, node-links perform far better than matrices on this task with regard to small sparse graphs. However, matrices outperform them about large and dense graphs." (§4 Discussion, PDF p. 12)

What it constrains about our design: "follow this credential to what it points at" — tracing an edge across the ACDC DAG — is a path-finding task in this paper's taxonomy, close to the operational center of what arcviz exists to let a viewer do. This is real evidence *for* a node-link representation over a matrix/adjacency view for that specific task, on graphs that are small and sparse (which most individual ACDC chains will be, per the corpus plan in `docs/research/PLAN.md` — an eight-deep chain or a wide fan-out is still nowhere near the size where this paper's matrix advantage kicks in). It is not, however, blanket evidence that node-link is the right choice for every task arcviz supports: the same paper's other six tasks (node-finding, link-finding, link-counting, etc.) mostly favor matrices at scale, so if a future affordance is "how many credentials does this issuer have chained under them" rather than "follow this one credential," this paper's own results argue the other way.

**[maceachren2012semiotics]** MacEachren, Roth, O'Brien, Li, Swingley, Gahegan, "Visual Semiotics & Uncertainty Visualization: An Empirical Study," IEEE TVCG 18(12), 2012. This is offered as the nearest analogue this stream could find to the brief's specific question — how the literature treats a graph node known to exist but whose contents are unknown — because a direct hit on that question, in graph-visualization terms, was not found.

The paper's typology treats uncertainty as multi-category, not a single dial, including a category directly adjacent to arcviz's "unverified": "MacEachren, et al [1] match nine types of uncertainty to these three components of information: (1) accuracy/error, (2) precision, (3) completeness, (4) consistency, (5) lineage, (6) currency, (7) credibility, (8) subjectivity, and (9) interrelatedness." (§2.1) Their experiments find no single visual treatment works best across categories: abstract sign-vehicles (e.g., an error bar) support faster judgments, iconic sign-vehicles (e.g., a stoplight) support more accurate aggregated judgments, and which wins depends on which uncertainty category and which information component (space/time/attribute) is being signified (§5).

What it constrains about our design: this is evidence, by analogy rather than direct hit, that a single "greyed-out" or "dashed-outline" treatment applied uniformly to every non-fully-disclosed node is unlikely to be the right move — the paper's own results say different uncertainty categories want different sign-vehicles, and arcviz has to signify at least four categorically distinct things (absent, undisclosed, redacted, unverified) that this framework would treat as different uncertainty types (roughly: completeness for absent/undisclosed, consistency or subjectivity for redacted, credibility for unverified), not one. **This is explicitly an analogy, not a finding about DAGs or credential graphs** — the paper is about discrete point symbols on maps and in tables, not about graph layout, and this survey found nothing in the graph-drawing literature that addresses partially-disclosed-node visualization directly. That absence is itself worth recording: **arcviz's central visual problem — representing a graph in which node existence is certain but node content is not — does not appear to have dedicated treatment in the graph-readability literature this stream could locate.**

**[purchase1997aesthetic]** — *lead, not verified.* Purchase, "Which Aesthetic Has the Greatest Effect on Human Understanding?", Graph Drawing 1997. Widely cited (including inside Ghoniem et al.'s own introduction, which this stream did read) as establishing that minimizing edge crossings has the largest effect on graph-reading performance among standard graph-drawing aesthetics. The primary text is paywalled at Springer and a free copy was not located. **We rely on it only insofar as Ghoniem et al. characterize it in their own introduction — a secondary report inside a primary source we did read — not as a direct citation of Purchase's own words**, per EVIDENCE.md's ban on citing a summary as though it were the source.

## Cluster 4: Design theory we will be held to

**[norman_jnd_preface_doet2013]** and **[norman_affordances_and_design]**, Don Norman, jnd.org essays (the preface to the 2013 revised edition of *The Design of Everyday Things*, and a standalone essay on the term's history). These were read directly and are quoted from Norman's own words, not from the book itself, which this stream did not obtain — see the `doet_revised_2013` lead entry in sources.lit.yaml. Treat the following as verified statements of Norman's terminology, and treat anything attributed to the book with a specific chapter/page beyond these two essays as unread.

The 2013 distinction, in Norman's own words: "Affordances define what actions are possible. Signifiers specify how people discover those possibilities: signifiers are signs, perceptible signals of what can be done. [...] The first edition had a focus upon affordances, but although affordances make sense for interaction with physical objects, they are confusing when dealing with virtual ones. [...] Signifiers are of far more importance to designers than are affordances." And on the term's origin and his own earlier imprecision: "The word 'affordance' was originally invented by [Gibson] to refer to the actionable properties between the world and an actor. [...] To Gibson, affordances are a relationship. [...] I should have used the term 'perceived affordance,' for in design, we care much more about what the user perceives than what is actually true."

What this constrains about our design vocabulary, precisely: an *affordance* is the actual possibility of an action (a SAID *can* be copied, a redacted field *cannot* be un-redacted by clicking on it) — it exists whether or not the interface communicates it. A *signifier* is the perceivable cue that tells the user the affordance is there (or isn't). **These are not synonyms, and the common informal usage — "affordance" meaning "a UI element that looks actionable" — is actually the signifier, or more precisely the perceived affordance.** Getting arcviz's own affordance-inventory phase (`docs/research/PLAN.md` Phase 4) right depends on keeping this distinction: the inventory's "signifier" column should name the perceivable cue, and the "affordance" column should name what is actually possible, and these can come apart (a redacted field might *look* clickable — a signifier with no matching affordance — which is precisely the kind of mismatch Norman's framework exists to catch). Separately, Norman's own essay on absence-as-signifier is directly relevant to how arcviz should treat undisclosed nodes: "even for items that signify by their absence, as the lack of crowds on a train platform" [norman2008signifiers] — meaning a deliberately-empty rendering of an undisclosed node is a legitimate signifier in its own right, not merely a failure to render something, provided its absence is unambiguous rather than looking like a loading state or an error.

**[nielsen1994heuristics]** Nielsen (NN/g), "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design." Two heuristics are load-bearing for arcviz specifically. Heuristic 1: "The design should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within a reasonable amount of time." Heuristic 2: "The design should speak the users' language. Use words, phrases, and concepts familiar to the user, rather than internal jargon." What this constrains: the disclosure-state distinctions (absent/undisclosed/redacted/unverified) have to be visible as *system status*, continuously, not just discoverable on inspection — and the vocabulary used to signify them has to be legible to the credential-holder's or verifier's own mental model, not KERI/ACDC internal terminology (a verifier does not know what "compact" or "graduated disclosure" means; they need to know what they can and cannot trust about what they're looking at).

**[nngroup_progressive_disclosure]** NN/g, "Progressive Disclosure." "Progressive disclosure defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making applications easier to learn and less error-prone." Directly names the pattern behind arcviz's planned "expand a card to full detail" and "toggle raw vs. pretty field rendering" affordances (`docs/research/PLAN.md` Phase 4). Constrains the design of the disclosure toggle: progressive disclosure is a legitimate pattern for *complexity* (advanced/rarely-used features), but it must not be reached for as the solution to *disclosure-state honesty*. Deferring "this field is redacted" to a secondary screen the user must actively navigate to would misuse the pattern — that information is not an advanced feature to hide, it is system status that heuristic 1 above says must stay visible.

**[tufte1983vdqi]** — *lead, not verified.* Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*. Widely quoted phrases ("graphical excellence is that which gives to the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space"; "above all else show the data") are attributed to this book across multiple quote-aggregator sites, none of which agree on a page number — itself evidence that none of those sites are quoting from a read of the primary text. Per EVIDENCE.md rule 6, this stream declined to launder an aggregator's citation into a source record and instead leaves the book as an unread lead. If Tufte's static/print-case guidance is needed for the synthesis phase, someone needs to actually read the relevant chapter and quote it directly.

**Nielsen's original 1990/1994 heuristic-derivation studies, and the "gulf of execution / gulf of evaluation" concept from Norman's earlier *User Centered System Design* (1986) / DoET itself** were sought as primary sources and not obtained in readable form within this pass; the "gulf" vocabulary in particular is well-attested by secondary description (multiple course notes and later papers restate it consistently) but this stream did not verify it against Norman's own words the way it did for affordance/signifier, so it is not given a source key here and should not be quoted as if verified.

## Claims this literature forbids us from making

- **"A small icon or muted color will tell the user this is unverified/redacted/absent."** [schechter2007emperor] and [dhamija2006phishing] are direct evidence that passive, easily-missed indicators do not register with a large fraction of users, even in a task where getting it wrong has real stakes (online banking).

- **"If we just add a warning label, users will understand what's wrong."** [felt2015sslwarnings] shows a professional team, applying warning-literature best practice deliberately, could not raise comprehension of *why* a warning fired — only adherence (what users then did) moved. Any claim that a warning treatment will make disclosure state *understood*, rather than merely change behavior around it, is not supported.

- **"Users will notice if we quietly omit the unverified/redacted signal in some view (e.g. a compact card, a print layout, a shared screenshot)."** The same two indicator-failure studies apply directly: an *absent* indicator is the specific failure mode they document, not a hypothetical one.

- **"Node-link is simply the right way to draw an ACDC graph" (unqualified).** [ghoniem2005readability] supports node-link specifically for path-following at the small/sparse scale most individual chains will have — it does not support node-link generally; the same paper's results argue for matrix representations on other tasks (node-finding, link-counting) as graphs scale up.

- **"The graph-visualization literature tells us how to draw a node whose contents are unknown."** It doesn't, as far as this survey could find. [maceachren2012semiotics] is the nearest analogue (uncertainty visualization for point symbols, not graph nodes) and is cited as an analogy, not a direct answer. This is a genuine gap arcviz's design phase has to solve rather than adopt from prior art.

- **"Affordance" as a synonym for "a UI element that looks actionable."** [norman_jnd_preface_doet2013] and [norman_affordances_and_design], in Norman's own words, make this the *signifier* (or *perceived affordance*), not the affordance. The affordance is the actual possibility of the action, independent of whether it's perceivable. Using the terms interchangeably in the Phase 4 affordance inventory would defeat the point of building that table.

- **"mDL users understand what they have and haven't disclosed" (in either direction).** No verified empirical source for this was found in this survey (see cluster 1). We can neither claim over-disclosure is a solved problem in shipped mDL UX, nor claim it is empirically demonstrated to be unsolved — the honest position is that this stream did not find the study.

- **Anything specific attributed to Tufte with a page number, or to Purchase's own text, or to Norman's DoET chapters beyond the two jnd.org essays quoted above.** All are leads in `refs/sources.lit.yaml`, not read this pass; do not upgrade them to cited claims without someone actually reading the primary text and recording a real locator.
