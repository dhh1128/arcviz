# Prior art: focus+context, fisheye views, and degree-of-interest (stream 1F)

This report fills a gap Daniel Hardman identified in [literature.md](literature.md) cluster 3, cued by his memory of Thinkmap's Visual Thesaurus: that survey covered node-link vs. matrix representations, edge-crossing effects, and edge bundling, but never touched focus+context, fisheye views, or degree-of-interest (DOI) — the tradition the design phase's "focus-in-place" / "in-place expansion" mechanism ([synthesis.md §2.5](../../design/charrette/synthesis.md)) and the SC13 "compact-chip legibility floor" ([stress-board/README.md](../../design/stress-board/README.md)) sit inside, whether the design phase knew it or not.

Every claim below carries a key into [`../../../refs/sources.focus-context.yaml`](../../../refs/sources.focus-context.yaml), same schema as `refs/sources.yaml`, per [EVIDENCE.md](../EVIDENCE.md). Two sources are marked `status: lead` there — Lamping/Rao/Pirolli 1995 and Gutwin 2002 were ACM-paywalled and not independently fetched; both are characterized only through material we did read in full (Card & Nation 2002; Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson 2007; Hornbæk & Hertzum 2007), and that provenance is flagged inline every time, not left implicit. Retrieval date for everything below is 2026-09-14.

## The DOI formulation, verified

Daniel's recollection was correct, and it is now verified against the primary source rather than resting on memory. George Furnas, "Generalized Fisheye Views," published in *Human Factors in Computing Systems CHI '86 Conference Proceedings*, pp. 16-23, defines, in his own notation:

> "DOIfisheye (x|.=y) = API(x) – D(x,y) where DOIfisheye is, according to the fisheye model, the user's Degree of Interest in a point, x, given that the current point of focus is y, API(x) is the global A Priori Importance of x and D(x,y) is the Distance between x and the current point y." [furnas1986fisheye]

That is the exact formula, term for term, that the task brief recalled. Two things about it are easy to over-read and worth stating precisely, both drawn from the paper itself. First, this additive form is explicitly the *simplest* case, not the general one — Furnas footnotes that "the DOI function is not required to be positive" and that the strategy needs only ordinal properties [furnas1986fisheye]. Twenty years later Furnas restated the general form as `DOIFE(x|.) = F(API(x), D(.,x))`, where F is "some combining function that is monotone increasing in the first argument, and decreasing in the second" [furnas2006followup] — the subtraction is one instance of F, not the definition. Second, the 1986 paper's own worked example is a *tree*, where `D(x,y)` is instantiated as path-length distance and `API(x)` as `−distance-from-root`; Furnas is explicit that "A Priori Importance need not be structurally defined, like 'level' in a tree. It may be independently specified for each point" [furnas1986fisheye]. The formula is domain-general; the tree instantiation is one worked case among several the paper names (lists, DAGs, general graphs, Euclidean spaces).

The 1986 paper also reports the first empirical test of the formula, worth carrying forward as a number rather than a slogan: 20 subjects judging relative position in an unfamiliar botanical taxonomy scored 52% correct with two flat views, 64% with one fisheye and one flat view, and 75% with two fisheye views [furnas1986fisheye]. That is a real effect, on a real (if small, if artificial) task, from the paper that coined the term.

## The lineage

**Degree-of-interest trees (Card & Nation, 2002).** Read in full [cardnation2002doitree]. This is not a re-implementation of Furnas's tree formula but an extension of it in a specific direction: ordered fractional DOI offsets between siblings (so "second sibling from focus" and "fifth sibling from focus" get different scores, not the flat per-level value Furnas assigns), geometric size scaling driven by DOI, semantic zooming of node *contents* as size shrinks, clustered aggregation of low-DOI branches, and — load-bearing for arcviz — a hard constraint that the whole tree stays inside a *predefined bounding box*, with animated transitions between DOI-driven relayouts on every focus change. The paper's own survey names four prior focus+context strategies it combines: logical filtering (Furnas's original elide-below-threshold), geometric distortion (hyperbolic tree, cone trees), semantic zooming, and clustering of distant branches [cardnation2002doitree]. This is the direct ancestor of "focus-in-place / compression toward a legibility floor" — a DOI-scored, bounded-space, animated layout — though Card & Nation's version compresses continuously by DOI value rather than to a single fixed floor form.

**The hyperbolic browser (Lamping, Rao & Pirolli, 1995).** *Lead* [lampingrao1995hyperbolic] — ACM-paywalled, not independently fetched; characterized here via two sources read in full. It lays a tree out uniformly on a hyperbolic plane and maps that plane onto a bounded circular display, so magnification falls off continuously with hyperbolic distance from the focus; selecting a node re-centers it with an animated transition [cardnation2002doitree]. Card & Nation's own critique, from inside the DOI-tree lineage: it is "less suited for cases in which the DOI varies in discontinuous ways across the tree," and "only a limited number of links out from the focus node can be seen" [cardnation2002doitree] — geometric distortion trades discontinuous-DOI expressiveness for a mathematically guaranteed bounded display, which the pure Furnas filter does not give you for free.

**SpaceTree (Plaisant, Grosjean & Bederson, 2002).** *Lead* [plaisant2002spacetree] — the DTIC mirror returned an HTML error page rather than a PDF on this attempt; characterized via Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson's review, which we did read in full. SpaceTree adds dynamic rescaling of branches to fit available screen space, camera movement, and preview-icon summaries of unexpanded branches, with extensive zoom animation to keep users oriented [cockburn2007review]. In the one head-to-head comparison this survey located (Windows Explorer vs. hyperbolic browser vs. SpaceTree, same CHI'97 "browse-off" dataset), results split by task rather than crowning a winner: Explorer won on tasks needing multiple simultaneously-open branches, SpaceTree won on topological-overview tasks like viewing ancestors [cockburn2007review]. The paper won IEEE VIS's 2022 "Test of Time" award, for what that is worth as a reputation signal rather than an empirical one.

**What each contributes beyond Furnas, in one line each:** DOI trees add bounded-space guarantee plus animated continuous relayout; the hyperbolic browser adds a mathematically bounded display via geometric distortion, at the cost of discontinuous-DOI expressiveness; SpaceTree adds explicit rescale-to-fit plus unexpanded-branch preview icons, and empirically wins only on a specific task class (ancestor/topology queries), not universally.

## Thinkmap's Visual Thesaurus

Verified from the primary source: US Patent Application US20050171760A1, "Visual thesaurus," inventor Marc Tinkler, assignee Plumb Design Inc., filed 2004-10-08, publication 2005-08-04 [thinkmap2005patent]. This matters because it is **not** a Furnas DOI filter and **not** a hyperbolic-geometry distortion. It is a force-directed layout — the patent's own words: "the position entities move as if they have a magnetic repulsion with respect to one another, and the edges between them have an elasticity" [thinkmap2005patent] — closer in kind to a spring/energy-minimization graph-drawing algorithm (the same family Misue et al. call a "spring algorithm" and build their mental-map-preservation work against [misue1994mentalmap]) than to anything in the Furnas lineage proper. Selecting a new word re-centers the display with an animated transition: "it may move first quickly and then more gradually into place in the center of the screen, allowing the user to more easily track the movement with the eye" [thinkmap2005patent]. So Daniel's memory of the Visual Thesaurus is a memory of a different mechanism than the DOI formula it gets grouped with in casual conversation — a force-directed re-layout with animated re-centering, not a distance-based filter or distortion. That distinction matters directly for arcviz, because force-directed layouts are exactly the non-deterministic, re-layout-on-every-change technique that the mental-map literature below treats as the hard case, not the easy one.

## What the empirical record says fails

This is the part of the survey that matters most, and the record is considerably less kind to focus+context than either Furnas's own 1986 abstract or the Visual Thesaurus's popular reputation would suggest. Three findings stand out as load-bearing.

**Fisheye menus lose to plain hierarchical menus, on the exact task fisheye distortion is supposed to help.** Hornbæk & Hertzum ran a 12-participant, eye-tracked, controlled comparison of Bederson's fisheye menus against hierarchical and multi-focus alternatives, for both known-item selection and browsing [hornbaekhertzum2007fisheyemenus]. Read in full, primary: "for finding known items, conventional hierarchical menus are the most accurate and by far the fastest," and the eye-tracking data show why the distortion bought nothing — "participants make little use of the non-focal regions of the fisheye menu, though they are a defining feature of fisheye interfaces" [hornbaekhertzum2007fisheyemenus]. People were not reading the context region at all; the one visual promise focus+context makes — that context stays legible in place, so you never lose track of where things are — did not get exercised in the task that should have exercised it hardest.

**Target acquisition breaks under fisheye distortion, specifically because the focus moves the target.** Reported via Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson's review and via Hornbæk & Hertzum's own characterization of the same study, since Gutwin 2002 itself is a lead here [gutwin2002targeting] — [cockburn2007review] and [hornbaekhertzum2007fisheyemenus] independently describe the same result: "Gutwin (2002) found that participants' target-acquisition times and error rates in fisheye interfaces increase with the level of magnification. In fact, a non-magnifying lens performed better than the magnifying ones" [hornbaekhertzum2007fisheyemenus, quoting/paraphrasing Gutwin's own reported numbers]. The mechanism is structural, not a tuning problem: as the cursor approaches a target, the fisheye's own magnification moves the target's screen position, so "the distance the cursor actually needs to travel does not match the distance shown on screen" (Cockburn et al.'s own framing, [cockburn2007review]). Gutwin's proposed fix, speed-coupled flattening — reduce distortion as cursor velocity increases — is itself an admission that the base mechanism actively fights the pointing task it sits inside.

**The synthesizing review's own bottom line, after aggregating dozens of studies, is a flat negative on distortion generally.** Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson, read in full, are explicit that this is not one bad study: "many focus+context systems distort the information space, which is likely to damage the user's ability to correctly assess spatial properties such as directions, distances and scales. Even in non-spatial domains, evaluations of distortion-based techniques have largely failed to provide evidence of performance enhancements, and several studies have shown that fisheye views can damage fundamental components of interaction such as target acquisition" [cockburn2007review]. The review's own table of high-level comprehension studies backs this with specifics, reported via that review rather than independently verified: Hornbæk & Frokjær 2003 found a fisheye text view "encouraged faster reading, but that this speed was at the cost of comprehension" on essay-style questions [cockburn2007review]; Nekrasovski et al. 2006 found pan-and-zoom "significantly faster" than a distortion-oriented focus+context technique, contrary to the authors' own prediction [cockburn2007review]; Donskoy & Kaptelinin 1997 found zooming outperformed a fisheye view for icon drag-and-drop [cockburn2007review]; Baudisch, Lee & Hanna's Fishnet study found users generally preferred a plain overview to the fisheye view, whose reception "polarised opinions" [cockburn2007review].

The one clean positive result this survey located and verified directly is narrower than its own headline claim suggests. Schaffer et al.'s 1996 controlled study — read in full, primary [schaffer1996fisheyefullzoom] — found fisheye views 102s vs. full-zoom 161s (F=9.91, p=.01) and 6.3 vs. 10.9 zoom actions (F=18.29, p=.00) for a hierarchical telephone-network repair task, a real and clean effect. But the same paper reports, unprompted, that only 70% of subjects completed the task successfully *regardless of view method* (F=2.32, p=.15, no significant difference by view) [schaffer1996fisheyefullzoom] — the fisheye's speed advantage did not make people more correct, only faster at whatever they were going to do anyway. And their own control-room operators, brought in for face validity, wanted the full-size familiar detail at leaf nodes that the fisheye's space economy had traded away [schaffer1996fisheyefullzoom] — the same "budget inversion" objection the design phase's own precedent seat raised against a different technique.

**What this constrains, taken together:** the literature does not support treating focus-in-place compression as a free win for either speed or comprehension. It is credibly a navigation-speed win under some conditions (Schaffer et al.'s hierarchical-network task), and just as credibly a comprehension loss (Hornbæk & Frokjær), a preference loss (Fishnet), and a target-acquisition hazard specifically at the moment the focus changes (Gutwin). None of the studies located here test a *compression floor* of the kind [stress-board/README.md](../../design/stress-board/README.md) proposes (a fixed minimum legible form rather than continuous shrinkage toward zero) — that specific mitigation is untested in this literature, for better or worse.

## Layout stability and the mental map

Misue, Eades, Lai & Sugiyama, "Layout Adjustment and the Mental Map" — read in full, primary, from the 1994 ISIS technical-report preprint of the 1995 *Journal of Visual Languages and Computing* article [misue1994mentalmap] — is the source the design brief asked for, and it earns the reputation. Its contribution is to stop treating "preserves the mental map" as a single intuitive property and split it into three independently definable, independently testable properties a layout transform can hold or break:

- **Orthogonal ordering** — up/down/left/right relationships between every pair of objects are preserved exactly (formalized via an N×E×S×W… direction matrix that must be identical before and after) [misue1994mentalmap].
- **Proximity relations** — "items which are close together should stay close together," formalized via proximity graphs (convex hull, minimum spanning tree, Delaunay triangulation, sphere-of-influence graph) that a transform can be said to preserve or not [misue1994mentalmap].
- **Topology** — the dual graph of the faces a drawing's edges divide the plane into; two layouts have "the same topology" if they share a dual graph [misue1994mentalmap].

Their own worked examples are exactly the failure mode a re-layout-on-focus-change design has to worry about: adding one edge to a small graph, run through a naive planarity-optimizing layout algorithm, "completely destroys" the user's mental map, because the algorithm has no notion that the previous layout was a thing worth preserving at all [misue1994mentalmap]. Their force-scan algorithm — push overlapping nodes apart along the line between their centers, using a spring-like force based on the gap between actual and "desirable" distance — is proven, with a formal theorem and proof in the paper, to preserve orthogonal ordering [misue1994mentalmap]. It is explicitly **not** proven to preserve proximity or topology, and the paper says so about itself, plainly, rather than claiming more than it showed: "we feel that the preservation of topology and proximity graphs needs significant further work. [...] The force scan algorithm ... tends to preserve some proximity graphs. This is important for the email visualizer... However, we have been unable to formalize this experience with a mathematical proof" [misue1994mentalmap].

**What this constrains for arcviz:** "the layout reflows but never reorders" ([synthesis.md §2.5](../../design/charrette/synthesis.md)) is a claim about orthogonal ordering specifically, and Misue et al. give arcviz both the vocabulary to state that claim precisely and a proven algorithm-family (force-scan, or the push-pull variant) that could deliver it under focus change. It is not, on this literature, a claim that proximity or topological relationships survive a focus change — those are open, "significant further work" as of the source paper, and nothing found in the broader search (Cockburn et al.'s review, the DOI-tree and hyperbolic-browser lineage) closes that gap since 1994/1995. Any claim arcviz's design makes about a viewer's spatial memory surviving focus-in-place transitions should be scoped to ordering, not to "the shape stays the same" — those are different properties, and only one of them has a proof behind it.

## Animation and accessibility

Bederson & Boltman's 1999 controlled study — read in full, primary [bedersonboltman1999animation] — is the right shape of evidence to cite here precisely because its result is not uniformly positive, and the paper is honest about that rather than rounding up. Twenty subjects navigated a 9-node family-tree hyperlink space in Pad++, with and without a one-second animated viewpoint transition on every link-follow. Animation produced a statistically significant reduction in *spatial reconstruction* errors — subjects later asked to physically reassemble the tree from photographs made far fewer and far less severe errors after the animated condition (0.4 vs. 1.8 unweighted errors, p=.001; 0.4 vs. 3.35 weighted, p=.001) [bedersonboltman1999animation]. But animation had **no significant effect** on navigation speed, **no significant effect** on relational recall accuracy ("Who was Billy's mother?"), and **no significant effect** on subjective preference [bedersonboltman1999animation]. The authors' own explanation: animation aids *spatial* memory specifically, not *symbolic/relational* memory, and the two may be more independent than intuition suggests [bedersonboltman1999animation]. Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson's later review situates this as the field's clearest positive result on animation, while also citing two null results in adjacent domains — Chui & Dillon 1997 found no significant main effect of animation in hierarchical file browsing, and Donskoy & Kaptelinin 1997 found no task-time benefit for animation coupled with overview+detail, zooming, or fisheye views [cockburn2007review, citing those studies, not independently verified here].

**What this constrains:** if arcviz animates the transition when a card expands in place, the literature supports a narrow, specific claim — it may help a viewer later reconstruct *where things were*, spatially — and does not support the broader claim that animation speeds navigation or improves comprehension of *what things mean*. Those need independent justification if claimed.

Separately, and non-negotiably regardless of what the comprehension literature says: any animated focus-change transition is a WCAG 2.1 **Level AAA** obligation, not a nice-to-have. Success Criterion 2.3.3, verified from the primary W3C text [wcag233animation]: "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential to the functionality or the information being conveyed" [wcag233animation], because for a viewer with a vestibular disorder the reaction to unwanted motion is not mild — "dizziness, nausea and headaches," and in severe cases "potentially needing bed rest to recover" [wcag233animation]. This is Level AAA rather than A/AA, which is worth naming rather than eliding, since it means it sits above the bar most accessibility statements commit to by default — arcviz would be choosing to meet it, not defaulting into it.

## The unknowable-distance question

This is the question nobody else in this literature has asked, as far as this survey could determine, and the negative result is itself the finding.

Every DOI formulation this survey located — Furnas's original, Furnas's 2006 generalization, Card & Nation's fractional-offset extension — requires computing `D(x, focus)`, a distance between two known points in a known structure [furnas1986fisheye, furnas2006followup, cardnation2002doitree]. The hyperbolic browser requires embedding the whole hierarchy in a hyperbolic plane before any focus is chosen, which presupposes the same thing: a known structure to embed [lampingrao1995hyperbolic, characterized via cardnation2002doitree and cockburn2007review]. Van Ham & Perer's graph-native extension of DOI (the nearest thing to "DOI for a partially-known structure" this search surfaced) assumes nodes' neighbors are *discoverable* by expansion, not *permanently unknowable* — an explored search of that paper found progressive reveal of real structure, not a structural commitment to a destination whose position can never be determined. A parallel search of the uncertain-graph-visualization literature (probabilistic graph layout, Network Hypothetical Outcome Plots, node-attribute-uncertainty work) turned up a different kind of uncertainty entirely: *probabilistic* uncertainty about whether an edge or node exists at all, encoded as blur, splatting, or animated resampling across possible graph instances. That is not arcviz's case. arcviz's blinded edge is not probabilistically uncertain — its existence is certain, cryptographically committed — its *destination's position in the graph* is categorically undefined, a structural absence rather than a probability distribution over possible positions.

No source located in this survey — the Furnas lineage, the DOI-tree/hyperbolic-browser/SpaceTree lineage, the empirical evaluation literature, or the uncertain-graph-visualization literature — defines DOI, or any focus+context behavior, for a node whose existence is committed but whose distance from any possible focus point cannot be computed because its position in the graph is structurally undefined. This should be read as **a confirmed gap in the literature searched, not a proof that no such work exists anywhere** — the search was not exhaustive, and a stronger claim would overreach what a literature survey of this scope can support.

**What this constrains for arcviz:** the composite design's own answer — draw a *frontier region* whose "declared property is that positions inside it do not exist" ([synthesis.md §2.2](../../design/charrette/synthesis.md)) — is not an adaptation of an existing DOI/fisheye technique to a new case. It is a genuine invention filling a gap this survey could not find prior art for. That is worth stating plainly to the design phase: arcviz is not implementing a known pattern for undefined distance, it is originating one, and the charrette's own convergence evidence (three independently-derived seats landing on "region, not point," per [synthesis.md §1](../../design/charrette/synthesis.md)) is the closest thing to external validation this technique is going to get, because the DOI/focus+context literature — forty years of it — has nothing to check it against.

## What this constrains for arcviz

Stated as constraints a designer can act on, not as a bibliography:

1. **The verified DOI formula is available to cite, but its 40-year track record on comprehension and target acquisition is mixed-to-negative, not the confident promise its 1986 abstract makes.** Do not claim focus-in-place compression will "help users understand the graph better." Claim, at most, that it may speed navigation under conditions resembling Schaffer et al.'s hierarchical-network task, and budget for testing target acquisition specifically at the moment of focus change, since that is the literature's single most consistent failure point [hornbaekhertzum2007fisheyemenus, cockburn2007review].

2. **The compact-chip legibility floor the design phase already committed to (SC13, [stress-board/README.md](../../design/stress-board/README.md)) is not merely a nice property — it is the one specific mitigation this literature never tests, because every fisheye/DOI system surveyed here compresses continuously toward zero rather than to a fixed floor.** That makes SC13's finding — the floor holds for identity and one deformation but not for the H6/H2 card-level tiers — a genuinely new empirical data point, not confirmation of something already known. Treat it accordingly: it is evidence, not folklore.

3. **"The layout reflows but never reorders" is a claim about orthogonal ordering, which Misue et al.'s force-scan algorithm can deliver with a proof behind it — it is not a claim about proximity or topology, which the same paper says explicitly is unsolved.** If arcviz's design language implies a viewer's spatial memory of *shape*, not just *left/right/up/down order*, survives a focus change, that claim needs its own justification; it does not inherit one from the mental-map literature as it stands.

4. **Do not attribute the Visual Thesaurus's behavior to Furnas-style DOI filtering.** It is a force-directed layout with animated re-centering — mechanically closer to the "spring algorithm" family Misue et al. build their mental-map work against than to any fisheye distortion or DOI threshold. If arcviz's design language invokes the Visual Thesaurus as precedent, it should invoke force-directed re-layout-on-focus-change, with everything that implies about proximity/topology instability under Misue's own framework — not a DOI filter's more localized, threshold-based redraw.

5. **Any animated re-layout on focus change earns, at best, a narrow claim: it may help spatial reconstruction later, not comprehension now, and not speed.** Ship the WCAG 2.1 AAA `prefers-reduced-motion` obligation as non-negotiable regardless of what the comprehension studies show either way — the vestibular harm is a different axis than the comprehension debate and doesn't wait on it being resolved.

6. **The frontier-region treatment of a blinded edge's destination is arcviz's own invention, not an application of prior art.** Say so, in the design documentation, rather than letting it read as if forty years of focus+context research already solved "what does DOI mean when distance is undefined." It didn't — this survey checked, and came back empty. That absence is worth recording as plainly as a positive finding would be.
