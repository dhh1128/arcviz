C is entirely underneath this card. Its SAID pill, its port, and any budgeted marker it carried are not small — they are gone. A removed indicator goes unnoticed (Schechter: 92% proceeded without one).
FAIL — SC3: "a focus interaction detaches the raised card from either parent or occludes a sibling's marker." Both, here: C is fully occluded, and A's edges attach to slivers.
FAIL — Rule 14 / P13.1: the two identical occurrences of ELBmpHyX1I… (edge n in B and F) must render identically and be inspectable; both are under the stack.
FAIL — topology: the edges marked ? attach to occluded geometry. At these offsets nothing distinguishes an edge landing on B from one landing on C — the reader cannot tell the short path from the long one, which is the one fact SC3 exists to show.
FAIL — z is not a graph dimension: stack order encodes recency of clicks, but reads as importance or validity ordering (AF1's forbidden semantics). The layout is asserting a ranking nobody committed to.
Verdict on the pile: REFUTED as a presentation of the DAG — not constrained, refuted. The argument is structural, not aesthetic: a pile is defined by mutual occlusion, and AF1's own conditions (a raised card keeps its diamond parents visibly attached; no occluded card's budgeted marker vanishes without trace) require that occluded cards remain partially legible at every point where an edge attaches or a marker sits. For any DAG in which an occluded card carries two or more incident edges — SC3's B and F, necessarily, since the diamond is the case — the pile must either reveal that card (at which point it is a spread, not a pile) or attach edges to hidden geometry (at which point the topology is unreadable and the render lies by ambiguity). The failure is necessary, not contingent on viewport or card count. What survives of Daniel Hardman's sketch is everything except the z-axis: the click, the one-card-at-reading-scale economy, and focus-plus-context — delivered in Panel 2 by in-place expansion, where compression does the work occlusion was doing, and compression, unlike occlusion, has a floor.
